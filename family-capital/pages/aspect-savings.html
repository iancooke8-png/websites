Step 5 of 8 · Build the structure

Savings

(the boring stuff that gives you freedom)

ISAs, cash reserves, emergency funds. Not glamorous — but liquidity gives you options that nothing else can.

How much cash is enough?

There's no universal answer. It depends on your income, your commitments, your risk tolerance, and how quickly you could access other assets if you needed to. We work out a number that makes sense for you — not a rule of thumb from a weekend supplement.

Too much cash is a drag on returns. Too little and you're forced to sell investments at the wrong time.

ISAs are underrated.

Tax-free income. Tax-free growth. No reporting requirements. Accessible at any time. The annual allowance is modest, but over decades, a well-managed ISA portfolio can be substantial — and completely free of income tax and capital gains tax.

We use ISAs strategically — particularly for dividend-producing holdings where the income would otherwise be taxed in a GIA.

Saving and investing aren't the same thing.

Savings are liquid, low-risk, and accessible. Investments are longer-term, growth-oriented, and come with volatility. Both have a role. The question is how much sits in each — and that depends entirely on your plan.

This connects directly to Step 6 (Investments) and to how your overall portfolio is structured.

Not everything has to be complicated.

Premium Bonds, NS&I products, fixed-term deposits. Sometimes the simplest options are the right ones. We're not too proud to recommend a savings account when it makes sense.

Junior ISAs and savings for the next generation.

Starting early matters more than starting big. A Junior ISA compounds for decades before your child even thinks about money. And for wealthier families, it's part of a broader intergenerational strategy that connects to trusts (Step 8) and tax planning (Step 7).

How this fits into your wider plan

Savings provide the foundation — the liquidity that makes everything else possible.
